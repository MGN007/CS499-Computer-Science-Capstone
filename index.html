01 · Professional Self-Assessment

Matthew Garcia Nieves · CS 499 Computer Science Capstone · Southern New Hampshire University

When I started the Computer Science program at Southern New Hampshire University, I did not have a specific career in mind. My employer offered the opportunity to earn a bachelor's degree without paying tuition, and I decided it was worth taking advantage of. At the time, I was open to the possibility of working in computer science, but I did not know much about what that would involve. Over the past three years, I have gained experience with programming, software development, databases, algorithms, and cybersecurity. Although I have learned a great deal, the experience has also helped me realize that computer science is probably not the career I want to pursue.

One of the biggest things I learned about myself throughout the program is that being able to do something does not necessarily mean I will enjoy doing it professionally. I have completed projects that required writing code, debugging problems, researching solutions, and learning unfamiliar tools. While I was able to work through those challenges, I found that I did not particularly enjoy spending long periods developing and troubleshooting software. I do not currently plan to pursue a computer science career, additional certifications, or graduate school. However, I still consider the education worthwhile because it gave me a better understanding of technology and helped me make a more informed decision about my future.

Throughout the program, I developed a better understanding of how important communication and collaboration are when designing software. In CS 319, I designed a mobile application concept that would help users make healthier food choices by scanning product barcodes. Before creating the wireframes, I interviewed potential users and developed personas to understand what information people wanted from the application. I also had participants test a paper prototype by completing tasks such as finding a product's health score and comparing healthier alternatives. Based on their feedback, I adjusted the design to make the health score more noticeable and kept the navigation consistent between screens. This project taught me that a design can make sense to the person creating it but still need changes based on how other people actually use it. Although this was an individual project, working with participants during interviews and testing showed me how involving other people can lead to better design decisions.

Another CS 319 project involved designing an interface for Kiva borrowers using flip phones. The limited screen space meant I had to decide which information was most important and how to display it without making the application difficult to navigate. I focused on funding progress, loan amounts, and repayment information because those were the details borrowers needed most. I also considered how lenders would need a different interface because they would be comparing loan opportunities rather than checking the status of a single loan. These projects helped me understand that communicating with users and considering different audiences are important parts of developing a useful computing solution.

My understanding of software engineering and databases improved through CS 465, where I developed the Travlr Getaways full-stack application. The project included a traditional Express website, an Angular single-page application, and a MongoDB database. One of the more challenging parts was understanding how the frontend, backend, and database communicated with one another. I worked with APIs and JSON to transfer information between the different parts of the application and used Postman to test operations such as retrieving, adding, updating, and deleting trip records. I also worked with authentication and protected routes using JSON Web Tokens. This project helped me understand how the individual concepts I had learned in earlier courses fit together in a larger application.

Algorithms and data structures were another important part of my education. Earlier coursework introduced me to structures such as vectors, hash tables, and binary search trees, along with the differences in how they store and retrieve information. I learned that choosing a data structure involves more than making a program work. It also requires considering efficiency, memory usage, and how the program might behave as the amount of data increases. I applied that understanding again during my capstone when I enhanced my ABCU Advising Assistance program. This helped reinforce the importance of selecting algorithms and data structures based on the problem being solved rather than simply using the first approach that works.

My cybersecurity coursework also changed how I think about software and information systems. In CYB 200, I completed several assignments involving security incidents at a fictional financial company. These incidents included exposed customer records, compromised credentials, and a fraudulent financial transaction. I examined how access permissions, employee decisions, and weaknesses in security procedures contributed to those problems. I recommended improvements involving least privilege, separation of duties, monitoring, and employee awareness training. One of the more important lessons I took from these assignments was that security is not just about having the right technology. Organizations also need effective procedures, responsible employees, and security measures that do not make everyday work unnecessarily difficult.

Developing my capstone ePortfolio gave me an opportunity to revisit projects from earlier courses and evaluate them with the knowledge I have now. Instead of starting entirely new projects, I worked on improving existing programs and explaining why those improvements mattered. This required me to look more carefully at how the programs were originally designed, identify weaknesses, and consider how changes could improve their functionality, efficiency, or security. I also had to document my decisions, review instructor feedback, and explain both what I accomplished and what I would still improve. This process helped me recognize areas where my technical understanding had grown since I originally completed the projects.

The first artifact in my ePortfolio is the Inventory Tracker application from CS 360 Mobile Architecture and Programming. I selected this project for the software design and engineering category. The original application used Java, Android Studio, and SQLite to manage inventory records. For my capstone, I improved the application by adding inventory searching, quantity validation, stock status indicators, and improved low-stock notifications. I also addressed a security weakness involving password storage by implementing salted PBKDF2 password hashing. These changes demonstrate my ability to improve an existing application while considering functionality, usability, and security.

The second artifact is my ABCU Advising Assistance program from CS 300, which represents algorithms and data structures. The original program was designed to load course information, display available courses, and show prerequisites. For my enhancement, I expanded its ability to work with prerequisite relationships by adding checks for missing prerequisites and circular dependencies. I also implemented a recommended course order using a topological sorting approach. This enhancement demonstrates how algorithms can be used to solve a more complicated problem and how selecting appropriate data structures affects the design of a program.

The third artifact is the Grazioso Salvare Animal Shelter project from CS 340, which represents database development. This project uses Python and MongoDB to manage animal shelter information. For my enhancement, I focused on improving database-related functionality and security. I moved database credentials out of the source code, added connection testing and input validation, created a compound index intended to improve filtering performance, and added an aggregation query for summarizing animal records by breed. Although I did not complete every possible performance and authentication test, the enhancement helped me better understand how database design, security, and data integrity affect an application.

Together, these three artifacts demonstrate different areas of computer science and how they connect. The Inventory Tracker focuses on software functionality and security, the ABCU program demonstrates algorithmic problem-solving, and the Grazioso Salvare project focuses on managing and retrieving data. They also show the progress I have made since completing the original assignments. The improvements are not limited to adding features; they reflect a better understanding of why certain design decisions matter and how a program can be evaluated and improved.

Looking back at the program, I would not say that my career plans turned out the way I originally expected. I entered without a clear direction and remained open to computer science as a possible career, but my experience with the coursework helped me decide that it is not something I currently want to pursue professionally. That does not mean the time I spent earning the degree was wasted. I developed technical knowledge, improved my ability to research and work through complicated problems, and gained experience explaining my decisions and responding to feedback. Most importantly, I will be completing a bachelor's degree that I committed several years to earning. Regardless of what direction I take professionally, those are accomplishments and skills I can carry forward.
